Field Notes

The Scarlet Ibis: A Daily Spectacle

The dry season, running from January through April, is the best time to visit the San Juan Delta. Water levels drop significantly, making it easier to navigate the delta's labyrinth of channels by boat, and the reduced humidity makes jungle treks far more comfortable. Wildlife is easier to spot as animals gather around the remaining water sources, and the clear skies offer spectacular stargazing far from any city light pollution.

The wet season from May through December transforms the landscape entirely, flooding vast areas of forest and turning the delta into an inland sea of extraordinary proportions. For those who want to experience the full drama of the delta at its most powerful, arriving in October and November offers the spectacle of the river at peak flood, a raw and humbling natural phenomenon that few travellers ever witness.

Field Notes

40,000 km² of Wild Delta

The San Juan Delta and the broader Orinoco Delta system is home to over 600 bird species, making it one of the finest birdwatching destinations in all of South America. The scarlet ibis is the undisputed star of the show, and at dusk thousands of these brilliantly red birds return to their mangrove roosts in spectacular formations that stain the sky. Alongside them, anacondas, caimans, giant anteaters, and river otters inhabit the dense network of waterways and forest.

The moriche palm forests that dominate the delta interior are a world of their own, sheltering rare orchids, brightly coloured frogs, and the moriche oriole, a bird found almost exclusively in this ecosystem. Guided boat tours through the narrower channels reveal a hidden world of extraordinary biodiversity, and knowledgeable local Warao guides will point out details of the forest and river that most visitors would walk straight past.

03
Field Notes

The Moriche Palm: Tree of Life

The Orinoco River stretches 2,140 kilometres from its source in the Parima Highlands of Venezuela all the way to the Atlantic Ocean. Its vast delta, known locally as the Delta Amacuro, fans out across roughly 40,000 square kilometres of channels, islands, and flooded forest before finally spilling into the sea. The San Juan River is the delta's main navigable artery, connecting the interior to the coast and serving as the lifeline of the Warao communities that have called this water world home for thousands of years.

Travelling by motorised dugout canoe through the delta's smaller channels is the only way to reach the most remote corners of this extraordinary landscape. The experience of drifting through a narrow waterway with the forest closing in overhead, the sounds of birds and insects filling the air, and the occasional flash of a caiman's eyes at the water's edge is something that simply cannot be replicated anywhere else.

Field Notes

8,000 Years on the Water

The Warao people, whose name means "boat people" in their own language, have lived in the Orinoco Delta for at least 8,000 years, building an entire civilisation on the water. Their traditional diet revolves around the moriche palm, river fish, game, and the edible larvae of the palm weevil, a protein-rich delicacy eaten raw or cooked. A visit to a Warao community is a profoundly moving experience, offering a genuine window into one of the last great river-based cultures in the Americas.

In Tucupita, the small but lively capital of Delta Amacuro state, local restaurants serve freshwater fish dishes that reflect the region's Venezuelan and indigenous heritage. The town's waterfront malecón is the social heart of the community, where locals gather at sunset and tour operators prepare their boats for the next day's adventure.
